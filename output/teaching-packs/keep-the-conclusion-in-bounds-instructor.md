# Keep the conclusion within the evidence

**XiXteen | Instructor guide | Skill 08: Does the Logic Follow?**

Notice when a conclusion reaches beyond the people or results actually studied.

Suggested teaching time: 20 minutes. All scenarios and figures are invented for reasoning practice.

Preparation: about 5 minutes.

Level: Introductory. Audience: Secondary and adult learners. Prerequisites: None; learners compare a stated group with the group named in a conclusion.

## Learning outcomes

- Identify a change from the group studied to a wider group.
- Rewrite an overclaim without losing the result actually reported.
- Name evidence that would address the missing step.

## Materials

- One student handout per learner, or display the activities and use paper.
- Pens or pencils; the instructor guide stays separate from student materials.

## Preparation

- Read all three scenarios and their model answers.
- Print the handout or display one activity at a time. Explain that all scenarios and figures are invented for reasoning practice.

## Teaching sequence

- **3 min - Compare the two groups:** Learners complete the club warm-up. Ask them to underline who was asked and who the leader makes a claim about.
- **7 min - Repair the school-wide claim:** Pairs complete the grading-rubric task. Check that rewritten conclusions include both the art class and the reported split in answers.
- **5 min - Discuss the missing step:** Reveal the main answer and use the discussion prompts. Distinguish an estimate about a wider group from a claim that every member agrees.
- **5 min - Transfer the skill:** Learners complete the residents survey independently. Use the assessment criteria before showing the model answer.

Timings are suggested starting points, not measured classroom completion times.

## Useful words

- **Conclusion:** The statement an argument asks us to accept on the basis of its reasons.
- **Sample:** The people or cases actually studied.
- **Representative sample:** A group selected to reflect the wider population relevant to the question.

## Warm-up: A club speaks for everyone?

Invented scenario: Nine of ten chess-club members asked say they want a lunchtime tournament. The club leader says, 'Every student in our school wants one.' No students outside the club were asked.

**Produce:** Write one sentence identifying the jump and one narrower conclusion.

1. Name the group asked and the group covered by the leader's conclusion.
2. Write a conclusion that keeps both the surveyed group and the nine-of-ten result.

**Before you finish:** Does your rewrite avoid both “all club members” and “all students”?

### Model answer

The survey covers ten chess-club members, but the conclusion covers every student. Nine of the ten club members asked wanted a lunchtime tournament.

**Why this meets the task:** It preserves the observed result without extending it to unasked students or treating nine as ten.

## Main activity: The grading-rubric survey

Invented scenario: Eighteen of 24 students in one art class say they prefer a new grading rubric. The other six say they do not prefer it. The principal announces, 'Every student in our 600-student school prefers it.' No other students were asked.

**Produce:** Write a scope comparison, a supported conclusion, and one further evidence need.

1. Complete: 'The evidence covers ___, but the conclusion covers ___.'
2. Rewrite the conclusion as one sentence that the survey actually supports.
3. Name one additional kind of evidence needed before estimating school-wide preferences.

**Before you finish:** Does your rewrite retain the art-class sample and the six students who did not prefer the rubric?

### Model answer

The evidence covers 24 students in one art class; the conclusion covers all 600 students. A majority of the surveyed art class, 18 of 24 students, preferred the new rubric. Estimating school-wide preferences would need a representative sample across classes. Even that would not establish that every student agrees.

**Why this meets the task:** It narrows the conclusion to the evidence and distinguishes an estimate of wider preferences from a claim about every person.

## Exit ticket: A survey of park volunteers

Invented scenario: A town has 2,000 residents. Twelve of 15 residents at a park clean-up say they favor Sunday street closures. The mayor says, 'Most town residents favor the closures.' No other residents were asked.

**Produce:** Write three sentences: the gap, a supported conclusion, and a better evidence plan.

1. Compare the residents actually asked with those covered by the mayor's conclusion.
2. Rewrite the result without extending it beyond those surveyed.
3. Describe who a new survey should include to estimate town-wide support.

**Before you finish:** Have you noticed that even a claim about “most” can reach beyond an unrepresentative sample?

### Model answer

The survey covers 15 clean-up participants, not the town as a whole. Twelve of those 15 participants favored Sunday closures. A town-wide estimate should use a representative sample of residents, including people who do not volunteer at park clean-ups.

**Why this meets the task:** It does not assume volunteers reflect all residents, yet retains the information their answers actually supply.

## Check understanding

Mark each criterion met or needs another attempt. Accept equivalent wording.

- The sample and wider claimed group are identified accurately.
- The rewrite preserves the number, group, and limits of the reported result.
- The proposed evidence addresses the wider population without promising certainty about everyone.

## Discussion prompts

**Does a narrow sample alone prove a wider conclusion false?**

No. A sample can fail to establish a conclusion without refuting it. In this art-class case, however, six students explicitly disagree, so the claim that every student prefers it is false.

**Why would a larger sample from the same art class still leave a problem?**

Including more people does not by itself fix a mismatch between the group studied and the wider population.

## Common confusions

**A majority in a sample means a majority everywhere.** Ask how the sample was chosen and which population it can reasonably represent.

**A gap makes the whole survey worthless.** The survey still describes the people asked. Limit the conclusion to what it supports.

**Representative sampling proves everyone agrees.** It can support an estimate of a population pattern, with uncertainty, not a guarantee about every person.

## Adaptations

- **More support:** Use two boxes: 'Who was asked?' and 'Who is the claim about?' For the main task, expect '24 art students' and 'all 600 students'. Then retain '18 of 24' in the rewrite.
- **More challenge:** Ask whether the art-class result refutes 'every student prefers it'. Expected answer: yes, the six surveyed students who did not prefer it already contradict that universal claim. This goes beyond merely noting that the sample is narrow.
