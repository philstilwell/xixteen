Article 01 · AI & human judgment

When AI Gives Advice, Who Is Making the Decision?

Learn to spot the choices hidden inside a helpful answer.

A XiXteen critical thinking lesson · October 29, 2026

Inspired by Anthropic's research report, published October 27, 2026. The study summary is attributed below. The classroom example and activities are XiXteen teaching material.

In this lesson

  1. Start with the question
  2. What the study found
  3. Read the evidence carefully
  4. Work through an example

01 · The question

What do you mean by “better”?

Imagine your school asks you to choose a better lunch plan. You ask an artificial intelligence (AI) assistant for help. It recommends the cheapest plan and gives you a neat cost table.

The sums may be right. But did you mean “cheapest” when you said “better”? Perhaps you meant healthier food, shorter queues, or meals more students would actually eat.

Before checking the answer, check the question the answer is solving.

A useful distinction

A calculation works out a number. A judgment call chooses between options when the facts alone do not settle the choice. Deciding how much each lunch costs is a calculation. Deciding how much extra to pay for a healthier lunch involves a judgment call.

02 · The research

What happened when experts used AI?

In an Anthropic experiment, 50 analysts used an AI agent—a tool that can perform several task steps—to assess a fictional farming service. Hidden instructions randomly assigned the AI's preferences. It had to argue honestly.

  • Across four analytical choices, 86% of decisions matched the AI's assigned preference.
  • 77% of surveyed analysts reused an AI argument in at least one explanation.
  • 15% credited the AI in their explanations.

One task, one model, and deliberately strong preferences limit what this tells us about everyday use. Analysts who declined AI worked more slowly and made more serious errors, but that small group differed from the AI users. This does not prove AI caused the difference. Read Anthropic's report.

03 · Check the claim

Influence does not automatically mean error

The research paper explains that the choices had more than one defensible answer. Agreement with the AI was not a score for being wrong. Randomly assigning its preferences helped the researchers measure their effect on choices. See the paper, sections 1 and 3.

The AI's preference on the final recommendation had a weaker effect. In the main planned analysis, the evidence did not clearly separate that effect from chance variation. Earlier choices could still shape the numbers used to reach the recommendation. See the paper, section 1.1.

For a student, the question to practise is: “What reason would justify this choice?” A changed mind can reflect learning. An unchanged mind can reflect stubbornness. Neither tells you, by itself, how well someone thought.

04 · A worked example

Two tutoring plans. Two meanings of success.

This is a made-up classroom example, separate from the farming study. A school has enough money for one of two tutoring plans. Suppose both cost the same and the expected gains below are reliable.

Expected results under the two plans
MeasurePlan APlan B
Students helped10040
Average score gain per student helped10 points25 points
Total score gains added together1,000 points1,000 points

An assistant says: “Choose Plan B. Students improve by 25 points instead of 10.” That comparison is correct for students who receive tutoring. But it leaves out how many students receive it.

Another assistant says: “Choose Plan A. It helps 100 students instead of 40.” That comparison is also correct. But it leaves out how much each student improves.

The first answer treats a larger gain per person as the priority. The second treats reaching more people as the priority. Neither priority follows from the table alone.

Even the equal total does not settle everything. Is an extra point equally useful to a student already doing well and a student near the pass mark? Which students would each plan reach? Would either plan leave out students who need the most help?

A careful recommendation might be: “I favour Plan A because our stated aim is to reach more students. I would reconsider if Plan B were much better at helping students at risk of failing.” This gives both a reason and a condition for changing the decision.

05 · Build the skill

Six tools for checking an answer

1. Separate facts, assumptions, and values

A fact is something you can check: Plan A serves 100 students. An assumption is something the reasoning takes for granted: the forecast gains will hold next term. A value is a priority: reaching more students matters more than a larger gain for each student.

Write these on three separate lines. Then ask whether each has the support it needs. A value needs an explanation of why it should guide the choice; a forecast needs evidence about how likely it is to be right.

2. Name the missing comparison

“Twenty-five points is a big improvement” invites a simple question: compared with what? The starting score? A different plan? No tutoring at all? Each comparison answers a different question.

In real school data, scores rising after tutoring would not by itself prove tutoring caused the rise. Students might have improved anyway. You would need a fair way to estimate what would have happened without the tutoring.

3. Make the strongest reasonable objection

If you choose Plan A, explain why a thoughtful person might choose Plan B. Then answer that reason fairly. Do not invent a silly objection that is easy to dismiss.

For example: “Plan B may give struggling students enough help to pass. My preference for wider access needs to address that possibility.” This is a stronger check than merely asking the assistant to make your preferred plan sound convincing.

4. Say what would change your mind

Choose a specific finding that would matter. “New evidence” is too vague. “Reliable evidence that Plan B prevents far more students from failing” gives you something to look for.

You can also try a sensitivity check: change one reasonable assumption and see whether your recommendation changes. What if Plan B helped 60 students? What if its expected gain were only 15 points? A decision that changes easily needs a clear explanation of the uncertainty.

5. Test your explanation without looking

Close the answer you were given. Explain the choice to a classmate in your own words. Can you say why the comparison matters? Can you answer a follow-up question about who benefits?

You may feel certain that Plan B is best yet struggle to explain why 25 points per person should outweigh wider access. That gap tells you what to work on. Feeling certain and having a reason you can defend are separate things. This exercise checks your understanding; it cannot prove your judgment was independent.

6. Check whether your sources are independent

Suppose three classmates tell you that the school bus leaves at four. You then learn that all three copied one old timetable. You have three reports, but only one source of information.

For the tutoring choice, asking three people to repeat the same assistant's answer creates a similar problem. Ask where each reason came from and whether anyone checked it against separate evidence. Agreement is more useful when you understand how it was reached.

06 · Keep these ideas

All 20 key insights from this lesson

  1. Define the goal. “Better” needs a clear meaning.
  2. Check what a number measures. A gain per person differs from a gain across a group.
  3. Correct arithmetic can leave a choice open. Different priorities can justify different plans.
  4. Separate facts, assumptions, and values. They need different kinds of support.
  5. Check early choices. The measure of success helps shape the final recommendation.
  6. Experts can be influenced. The study's participants were trained analysts.
  7. Honest advice can still favour one option. The experiment did not require the AI to lie.
  8. Agreement is not an error rate. Matching the AI does not prove a choice was wrong.
  9. Track where your reasons came from. Repeating an explanation is different from testing it.
  10. Confidence is not a check on reasoning. Try explaining and defending the answer.
  11. Look for independent evidence. Several reports from one source are not several separate checks.
  12. Ask for the missing comparison. “More” and “better” always invite “compared with what?”
  13. Check causes. Improvement after tutoring does not alone show improvement because of tutoring.
  14. Face the strongest objection. A fair challenge helps you assess your own answer.
  15. Check who gains and who misses out. A total can hide the distribution of benefits.
  16. Test your assumptions. See whether a reasonable change would alter your recommendation.
  17. Set conditions for changing your mind. Name the evidence that would matter.
  18. Keep claims within the evidence. One experiment cannot establish how all AI use works.
  19. Assess each part of the help. Useful calculations still leave goals and priorities to examine.
  20. Explain your decision. State your goal, evidence, tradeoff, and remaining uncertainty.

07 · Try it yourself

Pause before opening each answer

Write a sentence of your own first. These questions test the reasoning, not your memory of the percentages.

1. Find the hidden assumption

“Plan B raises scores more per student, so it is the better use of the budget.” What extra idea does this conclusion need?

Show a possible answer

It assumes that the gain per student helped should decide how the budget is spent. That priority needs defending against other aims, such as helping more students or reducing failures.

2. Correct an overclaim

“The analysts matched the AI's preference 86% of the time. Therefore, 86% of their decisions were wrong.” What is the mistake?

Show the reasoning

The percentage counts agreement with an assigned preference. It does not measure correctness. To claim an error rate, you would first need a justified standard for deciding which choices were wrong.

3. Find the next useful fact

You care most about helping students at risk of failing. What would you ask before choosing a tutoring plan?

Show a possible answer

Ask how many such students each plan would reach and how likely each plan is to help them pass. An average gain for all participating students does not answer those questions.

4. Make a recommendation you can defend

Choose A, choose B, or explain why you need more information. Complete these sentences:

  • Our main goal is…
  • The evidence relevant to that goal is…
  • My recommendation gives up or risks…
  • I would reconsider if…
Check your reasoning

Several answers can be reasonable. A strong answer connects its evidence to a stated goal, acknowledges a real drawback, and names information that could change the choice. “The AI said so” does not explain those connections.

For teachers: Ask pairs to defend different plans using the same table. Then have them identify whether they disagree about a fact, an assumption, or a priority. Link the discussion to XiXteen skills 04, 06, 11, 12, 15, and 16 in the teacher resources.
